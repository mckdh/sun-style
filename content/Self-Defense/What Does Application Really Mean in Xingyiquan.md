---
title: "What Does “Application” Really Mean in Xingyiquan?"
---
Walk into most traditional martial arts schools today, and you will usually see slow routines focused on gentle exercise and staying healthy. Staying fit and moving smoothly is great. But too many students spend years memorizing forms without ever learning what those punches, steps, and turns were built to do in a real fight.

A fair question people often ask me is simple:

> **"If combat applications matter so much, what do you actually teach in Xingyiquan?"**

To answer that, you have to look past the surface movements. In our school, practical application always comes down to two things: **the technique** (where you strike or pull) and **the engine** (the body mechanics that make it work against full resistance).

---

## 1. The Visible Layer: What Does This Movement Do?

Xingyiquan is built around the **Five Elements** (五行拳: *Pi, Beng, Zuan, Pao, Heng*) and the **Twelve Animal Forms** (十二形). 

If you just watch someone run through these forms, it is almost impossible to guess what the movements are for.

Take the **Dragon Form** (龍形, *Longxing*). There is a moment where you twist your torso and drop low toward the floor. To someone watching from the side, it looks like an awkward yoga pose or a strange dance step. You would never guess how it works in a fight.

![[main-fun-image.png|380]]
(The Dragon Form)

Underneath that strange pose is a very direct physical target. In class, I teach it like this: you grab an opponent's forearm, pull hard to snap their posture forward, and drive your rear heel straight down into the space right above their kneecap. When you drop your full body weight into that joint, the leg bends the wrong way and breaks.

Once you know the target, the movement stops looking like a dance. Every limb has a specific job.

This is true across Taijiquan, Baguazhang, and Xingyiquan. You cannot understand an internal move just by copying the outer shape. Old teachers guarded these targets carefully and only showed them to students they trusted. Over time, many practitioners only learned the outer routines (*Taolu*, 套路). The shell survived, but the practical combat knowledge was lost.

---

## 2. The Deeper Layer: What Makes It Work?

Knowing where to grab or step is still only the first step. 

That just answers *what* the movement does. The harder problem is:

> **What makes the technique work when someone is bigger, faster, and shoving back?**

This is where Xingyiquan gets practical. In internal arts, the physical engine behind every strike is called **Neijin** (內勁, internal power).

People hear the word "internal" and usually make one of two mistakes:
* **The Fantasy View:** They picture shooting energy fireballs or knocking people down across the room without touching them.
* **The Cynical View:** They write it all off as a fake martial arts scam made for movies.

![[Pasted image 20261003160554.png|260]]
(Internal power is not throwing video-game fireballs, and it is not a fake magic trick either.)

Real Neijin is basic physics and body structure.

Think of throwing a baseball. If you only throw with your arm and bicep, your shoulder hurts and the ball has no real speed. A good pitcher pushes their back foot off the rubber, turns their hips, whips their torso, and transfers their entire body weight straight through their fingertips into the ball. 

Pushing a stalled car uses the same idea. You do not push a two-ton car by flexing your arms. You brace your legs into the pavement, lock your spine, lean your weight forward, and push with your legs.

Neijin is that exact same connection trained into your entire body:
* Aligning your bones so force transfers without leaking
* Dropping stiff shoulder and chest tension (*Song*, 放鬆)
* Linking your feet, waist, spine, and hands into a single solid piece
* Taking rebound force from the ground and sending it forward

Knowing a technique without this body connection is like putting a fiberglass sports car shell on top of a lawnmower engine. It looks impressive standing still, but it has no power when you hit the gas.

Learning a martial arts trick takes two minutes. Rebuilding how your body creates, delivers, and takes force takes daily practice.

---

## Still Training Every Day

I am not an ancient master who lives on a mountain and knows every secret. I still train every single day, and every time I work with my master, I feel how much deeper this art goes. I run into my own stiff spots and bad habits all the time.

What I can offer is the clear training progression passed down in our lineage. We do not bury beginners under confusing mystical talk. We start by fixing your posture, teaching your spine and hips to move together, and testing each move against safe partner resistance. Once the physical structure is in place, the rest makes sense on its own.

Learning what a move hits is fun. But building the body engine so the movement actually works in the real world is where real training begins.
